# Research note: how African institutions, scholars and community practices document and transmit knowledge

Finding T05 of `docs/editorial/remediation-plan-2026-09-30.md`, phase P1.

## Status and limits

- Date: 2026-09-30. Desk research by an agent using web search and page fetching.
- What this is: a record of what was actually opened, with its context, its limits and one proposed adaptation per item. It feeds the editorial guide; it does not amend it.
- What this is not:
  - **Not a consultation.** No griot/djeli, elder, family, community, institution or specialist was contacted. Nothing here speaks for any community. The proposals need situated input (last section) before adoption.
  - **Not a survey of African methods.** There is no single African method, and European-trained methods are not one thing either. Nine items were opened, mostly West African and Sahelian or general-purpose frameworks. Central, Eastern, Southern and North African practice is essentially absent.
  - **Not a ranking.** Scientific and linguistic tools stay useful for asking "is this attested, by whom, when". Ross and Spear below show that even within academic practice the tools are contested. None of them is the sole arbiter, and none is dismissed.
- Reading caveat: the fetch tool returns a summary produced by a small model, not verbatim text. Full-text PDFs were re-read locally with `pdftotext` (Hampâté Bâ, Ross, the South African code). For the web pages, "read" means the fetched page content as summarised. Quotations are limited to a few words.
- The operator is a popular educator, not a scientist, linguist or historian. Nothing here should be used to suggest otherwise.

## Adaptations proposed for the guide

Each is a proposal for review, traced to a row above. None has been adopted.

1. **Source line records the chain, as given** (A-1): who taught whom, place, language, and whether the carrier names the source. Never inferred.
2. **Carrier's own additions stay labelled** (A-2) and **roles are named only if the source states them** (A-3, H-1); no one is presented as the voice of a whole people.
3. **Accounts are local, not continental** (B-1, B-2): attributed to place, language and carrier; an insider reflective account is cited as testimony to something specific.
4. **Function of the telling is recorded next to any datable claim** (C-1); cross-checking against written or archaeological sources is labelled as such, not as proof (C-2).
5. **Consent record before publication** (D-1, G-1): consent given, scope, by whom, withdrawal route. Absent that, use only what the carrier or community has already made public, and say so.
6. **Credit carrier and community in the source line** (D-2).
7. **Provenance and permission fields modelled on the Local Contexts idea, with "not stated" as a valid value; the project invents no restriction or label** (E-1, E-2).
8. **Restricted material is not published because a copy exists** (F-1); **competing accounts remain separate records** (F-2).
9. **No ruling on a community's own account of its name** (G-2); **carrier reserve recorded as "not shared"**, not filled from elsewhere (J-1).
10. **When a source's protocol is unknown, default to not publishing and to asking** (I-1).
11. **Endogenous knowledge is cited as a source in its own right** (K-1), pending reading the book.

## Open questions needing situated input

These cannot be answered from desk research. They need people with standing in the relevant communities or institutions.

- Which consent and attribution forms do carriers and communities in the peoples the project covers actually accept, and in which language, and orally or in writing? The South African draft (D) and UNESCO principles (G) are general; a specific community may differ.
- Is a chain-of-transmission line acceptable to carriers in every case, or do some carriers regard naming the chain as its own protocol (for example, as something given only to initiates)? Item A suggests the chain matters where it is named, and also that some knowledge is not shared with outsiders (its ritual-secrecy passages, including the 1928 episode).
- Would any community want Local Contexts-style labels or a Mukurtu-like access model, or do African communities and institutions already use other instruments? None was found in this research.
- What do CELHTO's and CODESRIA's own published methods say, once the records and the books can be read? This note offers no view on them.
- How should the guide treat oral accounts already collected by outside researchers (missionaries, colonial administrators, foreign scholars) where consent was never recorded? Items D and G point to a default of "not cleared", but this needs a ruling from someone with standing.
- Who, if anyone, may speak for a people's account of its own name where families and lineages give different accounts (compare F-2)? The project does not decide this; a named, situated advisory step may be required.

# Editorial personas

Version 1 — 2026-09-30. Operator intent and evidence are separate inputs.
Use with the [reader-facing register](reader-facing-register.md), the
[audience audit](../audience/audit-2026-09-30.md) and the
[measurement protocol](../audience/editorial-measurement.md).

These are **provisional needs-based personas**, not a demographic segmentation.
One person can occupy several of them. No fictional name, age, job, income,
ethnicity or family biography is required. The diaspora is the **primary intended
public**, as specified by the operator; analytics do not identify diaspora
membership. A location in France does not establish ancestry, and a location in
Africa does not establish local expertise.

## Evidence labels

- **Intent:** the operator's editorial priority, not an audience finding.
- **Observed:** a dated metric or a bounded comment sample, with its denominator.
- **Hypothesis:** a plausible need or motive that still needs direct validation.
- **Unknown:** no reliable evidence yet. Never fill the gap with a platform stereotype.

The evidence below comes from the September 30 audit. Site figures cover
August 31–September 29 and consented visits only. Meta/YouTube performance covers
September 1–28. Meta demographics describe followers, YouTube demographics views,
and TikTok demographics the selected seven-day viewer window. These populations
cannot be merged into a single profile.

## P1 — The diaspora reader exploring a name or a connection

**Priority: primary by intent.** The reader wants to understand a name, place or
story encountered in family life or public discussion, without needing prior
linguistic or historical training. This need is a hypothesis to validate, not a
claim that diaspora readers lack knowledge.

- **Observed:** Instagram followers: France 43.3%; site visitors: France 197/365
  (54.0%). Search has 106 measured visitors and 62 visitors opened a result.
  These figures support a discovery-and-exploration journey, not a diaspora count.
- **Observed qualitative signal:** one TikTok comment on the travel/“return” piece
  describes a visit and discovery without a wish to relocate. One comment does
  not represent a diaspora, but it directly challenges imposing a return narrative.
- **Need to serve:** a clear answer, a distinction between documented usage and
  possible origin, readable sources, and a way to explore a related name.
- **Friction to avoid:** shame about not knowing, a required “return to roots”,
  ethnic assignment from a surname, or an academic lecture before the answer.
- **Writing choice:** a concrete question, familiar words, the minimum useful
  context, then the competing explanations and where each comes from.
- **Next step:** open the relevant fiche/source or ask a relative about a usage,
  without requiring private family information to be shared publicly.
- **Validation:** invite voluntary readers to explain what they understood and
  why they came. Track source consultation once it is actually instrumented.
- **Unknown:** proportion of diaspora readers; languages; prior knowledge;
  personal research habits; whether family transmission is their main motivation.

## P2 — The reader comparing the piece with a lived or transmitted account

**Priority: co-reader of every piece about a people.** This reader may live on
or outside the continent. The relevant need is to recognise a usage, compare
accounts and be able to correct the project.

- **Observed:** Facebook follower geography: Côte d'Ivoire 48.3%, Guinea 11.5%,
  Mali 8.2%, France 6.7%. This is audience location, not proof of cultural identity.
- **Observed:** the bounded TikTok comment sample contains disagreement about
  Lingala and a response on Mali pointing to an earlier history. Neither comment
  establishes the historical claim; both reveal questions the explanation must handle.
- **Need to serve:** distinguish language, people, state and period; show the
  scope of a usage; leave room for oral and local accounts on their actual terms.
- **Friction to avoid:** treating a people as homogeneous, treating an outside
  book as the final word, or dismissing a testimony because it lacks publication.
- **Writing choice:** “This account is transmitted in [documented context]”,
  not “Everyone in this people believes…”. References identify the carrier and
  collector separately where applicable.
- **Next step:** offer a correction or another account with its context and any
  conditions on quotation, anonymity or reuse.
- **Validation:** examine whether replies add situated examples and whether the
  project can trace and review them. A correction is a contribution, not a failure.
- **Unknown:** how many readers hold local knowledge, whether they want to
  contribute, and which contribution route they find comfortable.

## P3 — The curious newcomer looking for an understandable explanation

**Priority: the entry condition for every public piece.** A person encounters a
post without knowing the project, possibly through a recommendation feed.

- **Observed:** Facebook views are 92.2% from non-followers; Instagram 79.7%.
  YouTube monthly viewers are 98.2% new and Shorts receive 91.7% of views from
  the Shorts feed. TikTok reports 96% For You traffic. These are distinct measures.
- **Need to serve:** understand who or what the piece concerns, what the question
  is, and what answer is actually supported, without prior episodes.
- **Friction to avoid:** unexplained terms, an author-first opening, an ambiguous
  “they”, or a provocative question whose correction is easy to miss.
- **Writing choice:** introduce the people/place and the useful distinction
  immediately. The hook must remain accurate when read alone.
- **Next step:** read the sources, save the explanation, explore a related
  subject or ask a question. No forced academic conversion journey.
- **Validation:** a reader can restate the central point and its uncertainty;
  compare early retention within the same platform and comparable post ages.
- **Unknown:** which wording caused a drop-off or misunderstanding. View counts,
  likes, completion and agreement do not answer that causal question.

## P4 — The reader who wants to deepen, transmit or contribute

**Priority: secondary service need and collaboration opportunity.** This can be
a parent, teacher, community knowledge holder, historian, linguist or simply an
interested reader. These roles are examples to recruit, not measured occupations.

- **Observed:** the Mande family page has 8 visitors and 235 seconds average
  time on page; the contribution page has 12 visitors and 12 seconds. The sample
  is too small to identify occupations or successful contributions.
- **Intent:** the operator welcomes specialist help while retaining an accessible
  public voice; oral expertise belongs alongside academic expertise.
- **Need to serve:** exact references, distinguishable accounts, known limits,
  a route to contribute and clarity about how a contribution will be reviewed.
- **Friction to avoid:** a bibliography that cannot be mapped to claims, missing
  oral provenance, inaccessible jargon or an implied endorsement by specialists.
- **Writing choice:** layer the detail: a clear explanation first, sources and
  deeper discussion next. Do not make an expert audience the default voice.
- **Next step:** consult a reference, suggest an alternative explanation or offer
  a documented contribution with permission to use it.
- **Validation:** ask volunteers to locate the support for a claim and submit a
  sample correction; measure source consultation and contribution completion.
- **Unknown:** whether parents, teachers or specialists form a meaningful share
  of the audience. A referral from an education domain does not establish a role.

## Reading contexts: needs are not places

Operator intent, 2026-09-30: the diaspora remains the primary intended public, and
readers in Côte d'Ivoire, Mali, Guinea and elsewhere on the continent are **direct
readers across all four needs**, not informants who merely validate a text written
for someone else. Any of the four needs can be held on the continent or outside it.

Four things vary independently and none is inferred from another: the reader's
**need** (this document), **where they live**, **which languages they read
comfortably**, and **how much they already know about the subject**. Only the need
goes in the brief. A location in Bamako does not make someone a local authority, a
location in Paris does not make someone a newcomer, and neither says which French
they read most easily.

What changes with the context is the _useful help around the same supported
claims_, never the claims. These are hypotheses to test with volunteer readers
(the measurement protocol's comprehension check), not findings:

| Need                         | What may help a reader who lives on the continent                                               | What may help a reader who lives elsewhere                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Explore a connection         | A named usage they can recognise, and the place and period it is documented for                 | The same usage with a note of where it is documented, without assuming a link to the reader |
| Compare accounts             | A clear way to say « chez nous on le dit autrement » and have it recorded with its context      | The same route, without implying that only residents can hold a valid account               |
| Understand a new subject     | The point stated without local knowledge being presumed or dismissed                            | Terms and place names explained where they occur                                            |
| Deepen, transmit, contribute | References usable offline or by message, and a contribution route that does not need an account | References that can be checked from a distance, and the same contribution route             |

French is written in common, clear French for everyone: short sentences, terms
explained where they occur, documented local terms kept and glossed. Do not imitate
a national accent or invent a « local French », and do not read a location as a
level of proficiency: French may be a first, second or learned language for any of
these readers. When a piece needs a French term specific to one place, give it as
that place's term.

## How agents use these personas

Before drafting, record in the existing working brief/report (no new mandatory
JSON schema): the primary reader need, the question answered, required prior
knowledge, what remains uncertain, and the helpful next step. A second persona
may be considered, but do not write four competing versions of every piece.

Every piece remains intelligible to P3; prioritising P1 never excludes P2.
P4 determines the usefulness of the references, not an academic tone for everyone.
Use the same evidential care in French and English without changing publication
locale settings.

For reading comfort start with mobile 320–430 px, then tablet 768–1199 px, then
desktop at 1200 px and above. Site mobile visitors are 83.0% of the measured sample;
YouTube mobile accounts for 89.0% of watch time. These support readable copy and
captions, not assumptions about education, attention span or device ownership.

## Validation before making personas more specific

Proposed research, not completed interviews: use a small voluntary first round
(for example 5–8 readers across the needs above), without calling it representative.
Ask what brought them, what they understood, what remained uncertain, where they
would verify it and whether they wanted to add an account. Record disagreement
and revise the personas. Do not infer sensitive identity from a name, country or
comment. Do not send invitations or contact anyone without the operator's instruction.

Review the persona evidence with each monthly audience report. Retire assumptions
that direct feedback contradicts; audience growth is not proof that a persona is right.

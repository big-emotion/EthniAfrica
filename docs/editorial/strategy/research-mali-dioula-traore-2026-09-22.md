# External research — Mali, Dioula/Jula, Traoré (2026-09-22)

Requested by the operator through `/ethniafrica-idee` as a research pass for a
short presentation, "Comprendre les sociétés africaines à travers leurs noms."
Its own idea — a name opens an inquiry into human relations, but its origin does
not define the current identity of its bearers — is exactly the caution this
research keeps returning to for all three names.

**This is not a single `idee` subject report.** The three names span three
different typologies (pays, langue/mot, patronyme) and the brief explicitly asked
to keep them separate rather than force a common origin. Instead, this note feeds
the "research readiness" step that
[the roadmap](roadmap-2026-q4.md) and
[the essay](../essais/comprendre-les-societes-par-les-noms-2026-09-22.md)
already assign to `idee`, for three already-scheduled research briefs:
**S1 (Mali, week 1), S3 (Dioula, week 2), S4 (Traoré, week 3)**.

Method: three independent, fresh research agents with live web search, one per
name, instructed to keep primary/specialist sources over Wikipedia (orientation
only), cite title/author/date/URL/page/paraphrase for every claim, preserve
competing etymologies without ranking by the origin of their author, and close
with an opening question, a verifiable sentence, phrases to avoid, and explicit
unknowns. Full agent transcripts are not reproduced here; this note distills them
and checks them against the corpus.

## What can be said now

- **Mali, 22 September 1960**: the ex-French Soudan proclaims independence as
  the République du Mali, a month after the Mali Federation with Senegal broke
  apart (20 August 1960) — a deliberate reclamation of the medieval empire's
  name, reinforced by Modibo Keïta sharing the founding dynasty's patronym.
  Solid, multiply attested, safe to state as fact.
- **"Mali" as the empire's/capital's name is clearly attested 1330s–1390s** via
  al-'Umari, Ibn Battuta (who visited the capital himself, 1352–53), and Ibn
  Khaldun. Safe to state, with the caveat that these three chronicles were read
  by our agent only in secondary summary, not full text.
- **The Manden/Mandé endonym is well documented** (Labouret 1934): Mandenka call
  themselves and their country Manden/Mandé; "Mali" is very likely an exonym
  relayed through Fula, Berber and Arabic forms — but no source dates or
  attributes the naming act itself.
- **The "hippopotamus" etymology for Mali is not safe to state as fact** —
  Labouret (1934) calls it "certainly to be discarded," and Wiktionnaire itself
  only offers it as an unclear hypothesis. **`MLI.json`'s `etymology` field
  currently states it flatly as fact** (see Corpus check below) — this is a live
  discrepancy, not a hypothetical one.
- **Dioula/Jula denotes three genuinely distinct, coexisting current uses**:
  a language (Jula, ISO 639-3 `dyu`), an ethnic/identity label concentrated in
  northern Côte d'Ivoire and western Burkina Faso (Bobo-Dioulasso, Odienné,
  Kong), and a purely occupational term elsewhere in the Mande world (Mali,
  Guinea, Gambia) — "you become dyula by going into trade." This three-way split
  is well attested (Person, Bazin, Cissé 2025, Sanogo 2024) and is itself the
  fact to publish, not a gap to close.
- **Julakan (jula + kan) names the trade vehicular language, not the "ethnic"
  Dioula identity or its own local languages** (kangɛ, Konyakakan,
  Odiennekakan, Maukakan per Sanogo 2024). Conflating Julakan with Dioula ethnic
  identity is a documented error to avoid — and the corpus fiche already gets
  this distinction right (see Corpus check).
- **Dioula ≠ Wangara**: related, overlapping Islamized Mande trade-network
  histories, but lexically distinct terms with distinct etymological proposals.
  The corpus fiche already states this correctly.
- **Traoré is an endogenous Mande jamu** (clan name), not an exonym: the only
  variation across "Tarawele" (Bambara) → "Traoré" (French colonial
  transcription) → "Trawally" (Gambian/Sierra Leonean English) is a transcription
  chain, not a change of who names whom.
- **Traoré as jamu spans at least three distinct, independently documented
  social lineages**: (1) a warrior/founding lineage from the Sunjata epic
  (Tiramakhan Traoré, general of Sunjata, credited with founding lines in
  Kaabu, Kala, Bendougou); (2) an Islamic scholarly/merchant nisba
  ("Tarawiri") among the Jakhanke and Dyula, attested by name from the 16th
  century (Bukari Tarawiri, qadi of Djenné); (3) the 19th-century Kénédougou
  royal dynasty (Tiéba and Babemba Traoré, Sikasso, resisted French conquest
  until 1898). **No source connects or excludes a link between these three.**
- **A shared surname is not evidence of shared descent.** The single strongest,
  most citable academic finding across all three reports: Gregory Mann (2002,
  "What's in an Alias?", _History in Africa_ 29) argues a jamu is "a ticket to
  mobility" — an instrument of social affiliation, alliance and status that
  creates bonds between strangers — not a closed genealogical register, and
  explicitly warns against reading the Sunjata epic as if it were one. This is
  exactly the caution the corpus fiche already states (`claimStatus: contested`
  on the etymology, jamu explained as clan-level not individual-level) and
  exactly the caution the presentation's own premise calls for.
- **Joking-cousinship (senankuya) pairs exist for Traoré**: Traoré–Koné
  (attested via McNaughton 2008, the "beans" joke) and Traoré–Diarra (weaker
  sourcing). These organize relations _between_ different jamu, and say nothing
  about kinship _within_ Traoré.

## What should wait for a documented episode

- **The exact linguistic root and original meaning of "Mali" itself** — every
  source consulted, including the best (Labouret), says this is unresolved.
  Do not assert an etymology; distinguish the proposed etymology from the first
  dated attestation, as the fiche's `middleAges` field already partly does.
- **Whether "Malal" (Al-Bakri, before 1068) is simply an older spelling of
  "Mali," or the name of a separate founding polity** — a 2018 archaeological
  reading (MacDonald, Camara et al.) argues the latter; not established as
  consensus. Say "disputed among specialists," not settled either way.
- **Which site was the historical capital** (Niani vs. Kangaba vs. Diêriba) —
  actively disputed; Kathryn Green (1991) argues the Kangaba identification may
  itself be a modern (Delafosse-era) reconstruction rather than continuous oral
  tradition. The fiche's `middleAges` field is already correctly cautious here
  ("la localisation de sa capitale n'est pas établie") — keep it that way, don't
  let a script re-assert Niani or Kangaba as settled.
- **The exact etymological root of "Dioula/Jula"** — six competing proposals
  (Manding "marchand," Soninké _jala_, Arabic _jawla_, a root _dyo_ explicitly
  rejected by a later scholar, Delafosse's disputed "noble origin" claim) with
  no consensus. Present as competing, not resolved.
- **Whether "jula" as a word predates Jobson's 1623 attestation** — the "first
  written mention" claim rests on one contemporary scholar (Cissé 2025); no
  independent check of earlier Arabic chronicles was possible in this pass.
- **Any claim connecting the three Traoré lineages (Sunjata-era warrior clan,
  Jakhanke/Dyula scholarly nisba, Kénédougou royal dynasty) into one continuous
  line** — undocumented; would be invention if asserted.
- **Any claim that two people named Traoré today share an ancestor** — the
  literature (Mann 2002) suggests this question is the wrong one to ask of a
  jamu system, not merely unanswered.
- **The corpus's own Traoré↔Diop cross-ethnic patronym-correspondence claim**
  (`PAT_TRAORE.json`, sourced to `gha-iv-mali`) was not found or corroborated by
  this pass's research (which instead found Traoré–Koné and Traoré–Diarra
  senankuya within the Mande world). These may be two distinct, both-real
  mechanisms (cross-ethnic pact vs. intra-Mande joking-cousinship) rather than a
  contradiction — worth a curator pass to confirm both hold up and are not
  conflated in any future script.

## Full agent reports

Each subagent report — with per-claim sourcing (title/author/date/URL/page/
paraphrase), full etymology tables, and its own opening question / verifiable
sentence / phrases to avoid / unknowns — was delivered directly to the operator
in the session transcript rather than duplicated in full here, to keep this file
a working reference rather than a second copy. Notable named sources surfaced,
beyond what the roadmap's S1/S3/S4 briefs already cite:

- **Mali**: Henri Labouret, _Les Manding et leur langue_ (1934); Nehemia
  Levtzion, _The Thirteenth- and Fourteenth-Century Kings of Mali_ (1963);
  Kevin MacDonald, Séni Camara et al., _The 'Pays Dô' and the Origins of the
  Empire of Mali_ (~2018); Kathryn L. Green, _"Mande Kaba," the Capital of
  Mali: A Recent Invention?_ (1991).
- **Dioula/Jula**: Chikouna Cissé, _Entre descriptions (pré)coloniales et
  descriptions de soi_ (2025) — the strongest single source found, reworking
  Delafosse, Tauxier, Binger, Person and Bathily with precise pagination;
  Richard Jobson, _The Golden Trade_ (1623, read directly, word-for-word);
  Mamadou Lamine Sanogo (2024 press piece, and 2003 book chapter reference).
- **Traoré**: Gregory Mann, _What's in an Alias?_ (2002); the _African History
  Extra_ blog on the Wangara/Jakhanke diaspora (Bukari Tarawiri, 16th c.);
  Robert Pageard (1961) via the Tiramakhan Traoré line; Jan Jansen's
  _Kamabolon_ work (identified but unreadable in this pass — scanned PDF).

Several primary sources (Al-Bakri, Ibn Battuta, al-'Umari, Ibn Khaldun, the
Timbuktu chronicles, Jansen's _Les secrets du Manding_, Cissé & Kamissoko's _La
Grande Geste du Mali_) were identified and located but **not read in full text**
by any of the three agents — either paywalled, or served as corrupted/scanned
PDFs the fetch tooling could not extract. A library or JSTOR/Cairn pass on these
remains necessary before any script asserts their content in detail rather than
in the hedged form used above.

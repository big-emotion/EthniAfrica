| PAT_MAMBA |         0.51 → 0.68 |               7 → 6 |                  3 → 5 |

**PAT_MAMBA.** The fiche already had a name history (birth: the 1935 census of
chiefs by N. J. van Warmelo; one hypothesis, from Richard Patricks, 2002, linking
the clan to the royal Dlamini). Tonight's second reading added what the sources
say and no more:

- The praise lines of the clan, from Patricks's entry MAMBA (p. 32), as an oral
  text collected from unnamed informants between 1976 and 1984: « nkhosi, mamba,
  ntfulini, mamba lendze lesensokonsokweni ». The book gives no translation of the
  lines and no origin story.
- A link with the Ginindza clan, from three observations: van Warmelo writes
  « Mamba, abaka or Ginindza, abaka » for one chief; Patricks's entry GININDZA
  repeats the line « mamba lendze leseNsokonsokweni »; and each entry bars
  marriage with the other clan. The sources do not say whether the two clans
  share an origin. The printed page of the GININDZA entry is not readable in the
  scanned text (between pp. 17 and 20) and is stated as such.
- Hilda Kuper, _The Swazi: A South African Kingdom_ (1964), p. 59: Swazi
  associate mambas with kings, harmless green snakes with commoners and women.
  Kuper does not mention the Mamba clan, so the page records the belief and no
  more.
- The homonym: the Zulu word _imamba_, « very deadly snake » (Colenso, 1861,
  p. 284). The sources do not say that the clan name comes from it.
- The alliances and social-function gaps now say what was searched. The marriage
  bars with Ginindza and Mabuza are not an alliance pact and are not entered as one.

Source ladder followed: (a) UNESCO's _Histoire générale de l'Afrique_ could not be
opened (unesdoc answered 403), so no chapter is cited; (b) Wikipedia was not used;
(c) Patricks 2002 and Kuper 1964 (academic, read in the Internet Archive text),
van Warmelo 1935 (archive), Colenso 1861 (linguistic reference). Bryant's _Olden
Times in Zululand and Natal_ (1929) was searched for « Mamba » and mentions only
people and places of that name, not the Swazi clan. No Arabic source applies. Open:
an origin story for the name with its carrier, bearers, and the relation between
Mamba and Ginindza, for which Matsebula's history of Swaziland and Bonner's
_Kings, Commoners and Concessionaires_ are the next books to try.
